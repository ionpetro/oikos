# Oikos foundations research — phase 1

Researched 2026-11-02. Research and proposed curriculum only; no course items authored. Scope: the first six units, with subjects `choices`, `margins`, `incentives`, `markets`, `incidence`, and `spillovers`. Every named learner-facing situation below is an original fictional teaching proposal. Numerical inputs are invented assumptions, not estimates of actual behavior.

## Recommended sequence

| Unit | Working title | Four proposed lessons | Skill at the end |
|---|---|---|---|
| u01 · choices | What are you giving up? | Identify the feasible options; Find the next-best alternative; Count time and money once; When the constraint changes | Identify a relevant opportunity cost without confusing it with every rejected option or a judgment about someone's priorities. |
| u02 · margins | What changes with one more? | Total versus additional; Compare the next step; Leave sunk costs behind; Refunds, thresholds, and bundles | Compare the consequences that actually differ between feasible next actions. |
| u03 · incentives | What does the system reward? | Map the reward; Notice the unmeasured task; Incentives can change meaning; Test the system before scaling | Explain a plausible response mechanism while distinguishing it from a demonstrated causal effect. |
| u04 · markets | Read the market, check the model | Demand is a schedule; Supply and a clearing price; Shift or movement?; When the benchmark is insufficient | Make conditional predictions, and identify what the facts do not establish. |
| u05 · incidence | Who responds—and who pays? | Responsiveness in percentages; Revenue is not profit; A tax creates a wedge; Incidence is not a policy verdict | Calculate a specified elasticity or price wedge and separate legal remittance, economic burden, and distributional evaluation. |
| u06 · spillovers | Who is missing from the decision? | Identify a third-party effect; Private versus social margins; Compare ways to change incentives; Limits, distribution, and voice | Include omitted effects and explain why a model calculation does not select a universally best institution. |

The order supplies prerequisites: marginal comparisons require a feasible alternative; incentive analysis requires marginal rewards; incidence requires market equilibrium and responsiveness; spillover analysis reuses margins and asks whose costs were left out. Introduce the distinction between a conditional model and an observed finding in u01 and revisit it throughout, rather than leaving it to a final disclaimer. [F1, F2, F3]

## Unit research and original teaching proposals

### u01 — What are you giving up?

**Source-supported core.** Opportunity cost concerns the next-best feasible alternative. CORE explicitly distinguishes that forgone payoff from direct costs, which helps prevent double counting. Noncash considerations can matter even when a teaching example uses dollars. [F1]

**Original scenarios.** Nia, a freelance designer, has one Saturday slot. A confirmed editing job pays $120 and requires $20 of materials; a second available task yields $70 net. She instead volunteers at a free community workshop. Ask for the forgone net cash alternative ($100), without asking whether volunteering is objectively the wrong choice. A separate college administrator has one room slot and chooses between two already-approved uses; room availability, rather than money, is the binding constraint. Held-back transfer: a candidate compares a paid shift with an unpaid caregiving commitment and must identify the unpriced consideration before recommending anything.

**Close contrast.** The $100-net job is cancelled before Nia chooses. Keeping the old opportunity cost would use an unavailable alternative. Another contrast permits doing both tasks at different times, removing the original mutual exclusion.

**Misconceptions to diagnose.** Free admission means no cost; add all rejected alternatives; use gross pay while ignoring avoidable inputs; the highest cash payoff proves the best overall choice.

**Boundary and ethical question.** Specify feasibility, time window, and whose preferences are being compared. Missing information can make the appropriate answer “identify the alternative first.” A person's constrained choice does not establish that their circumstances are fair. [F3, F15]

**Useful native practice.** Match resource to forgone use; sort feasible/unavailable alternatives; repair a double count; transfer to a new time constraint. Fieldwork: list two feasible uses of one limited resource and identify which facts could change their order.

### u02 — What changes with one more?

**Source-supported core.** Marginal analysis compares changes, not averages or historical totals. An irrecoverable past expense is different from a future payment or a refund that can still be recovered. [F2]

**Original scenarios.** A small translation cooperative can take up to four additional jobs with stated incremental benefits and costs (N2 below). A community event already spent $600 irrecoverably; the question is whether to spend a further $120 to receive $260, with all other consequences held equal. Held-back transfer: a team deciding whether to renew a software tool must separate last year's setup bill from this year's renewal and retraining costs.

**Close contrasts.** Add a $200 deposit that is refundable only if the event is cancelled: that changes the comparison. Add a minimum two-session package: checking the first session alone may miss the value of the bundle. A contractual cancellation cost is a future consequence, even though the commitment was made earlier.

**Misconceptions to diagnose.** Continue because we already spent a lot; stop because average cost is high even though the next step is beneficial; every fixed cost is sunk; incremental benefits must fall in every activity.

**Boundary.** A simple stop-at-the-first-negative rule needs the stated pattern of benefits/costs and no omitted complementarities, setup thresholds, or other constraints. Equality can create a tie; do not manufacture a unique answer where the inputs imply indifference. A financial comparison is not permission to ignore an independently stated obligation.

**Useful native practice.** Ordered procedure: identify live options, isolate differing consequences, include recoverable amounts, compare. Use typed arithmetic, sunk/recoverable/future sorting, and a changed-refund transfer. Fieldwork: identify one number in a real decision that is historical rather than changeable.

### u03 — What does the system reward?

**Source-supported core.** A reward for one measurable task can divert attention from another task when they compete for effort. Effects depend on measurement, task substitutability, risk, and the wider arrangement; stronger performance pay is not universally better. [F4] Incomplete-contract models identify information and specification limits, not proof that workers are lazy. [F6]

**Original scenarios.** A support team rewards tickets marked closed while customers care about durable resolution. Give two fully specified logs and ask what each measure counts (N4). A workforce college rewards signed employer agreements while students need usable placements: learners identify the unmeasured quality dimension. Held-back transfer: a shared kitchen announces a late-cleanup charge, and the manager proposes a reversible trial measuring both delay and staff burden; learners distinguish the hypothesized response from data that would support it.

**Evidence boundary case.** Gneezy and Rustichini's 2000 study observed increased late pickups after a particular fine in a small set of Israeli day-care centers. The authors explicitly reject claiming the same effect for every fine. Use it as a counterexample to certainty about deterrence, not a universal rule that fines backfire or proof of one psychological mechanism. [F5]

**Misconceptions to diagnose.** A higher target proves higher value; a correlation proves the incentive caused the change; everyone responds identically; a perverse incentive excuses falsifying records.

**Practical and ethical boundary.** Do not score a speculative “best bonus rate.” Score whether a proposal measures its intended outcome, acknowledges omitted work, and respects an explicit no-deception constraint. Put tradeoffs over monitoring, autonomy, and acceptable burdens in reflection rather than disguising them as arithmetic.

**Useful native practice.** Match reward to behavior made relatively attractive; contrast metric versus goal; sort observed/hypothesized/unsupported claims; repair a falsely certain prediction. Fieldwork: identify one rewarded measure and one valuable activity it may miss.

### u04 — Read the market, check the model

**Source-supported core.** Demand and supply describe quantities at different prices for a defined good, place, and period. An own-price change differs from a shift caused by another relevant factor. [F7, F8] Competitive equilibrium is a benchmark with assumptions about price-taking, comparable goods, information, and independent participation—not a description automatically true of every market. [F9]

**Original scenarios.** A fictional market for identical refill bottles has posted schedules and no large participant. Learners locate the clearing price, then diagnose excess demand at another price (N5). A material-cost increase shifts the supply schedule while buyer conditions stay fixed. Held-back transfer: the same quantities are sold by one supplier with substantial price-setting discretion; learners identify which prior benchmark assumption changed before predicting a price.

**Close contrast.** “The product's price changed” produces movement on its unchanged demand schedule; “a close substitute became more expensive” may shift demand when the substitute relationship is specified. If both supply and demand shift, the price direction may be unresolved without magnitudes. A price-and-sales change alone does not identify which curve moved.

**Misconceptions to diagnose.** A price increase shifts the same good's demand; demand means desire without ability to purchase; equilibrium means morally fair; one market transaction reveals a whole demand curve.

**Boundary and ethics.** Fix units, quality, period, and other variables. Let price adjustment take time; a model intersection is not evidence of instantaneous clearing. Ability to pay and moral need are distinct questions. [F7, F9, F15]

**Useful native practice.** Read a compact schedule; pair shock with the affected relation; sort shift/movement/insufficient information; transfer to a changed market structure. Fieldwork: state the market and the assumptions before explaining a price change.

### u05 — Who responds—and who pays?

**Source-supported core.** Elasticity compares percentage changes; it is not the slope measured in dollars per unit. The midpoint convention uses a common base for a finite interval. [F10] In the competitive model, tax incidence depends on relative responsiveness, while a per-unit tax separates the buyer's price from the seller's net receipt. [F11, F12]

**Original scenarios.** An invented demand schedule for workshop seats supports a midpoint calculation and a revenue comparison (N6). Two market exercises apply the same tax-wedge method with different responsiveness (N7–N8). A seller's invoice remits the tax, but the learner must compare the before/after buyer and seller prices rather than read economic burden off the form. Held-back transfer: the legal remitter changes while the hypothetical schedules, enforcement, and transaction costs remain identical.

**Close contrasts.** A lower price raises quantity but can lower revenue. Even a revenue increase need not increase profit without cost information. If buyers gain new substitutes over time, last month's elasticity need not govern a later response. Observational before/after sales affected by advertising do not isolate a demand elasticity.

**Misconceptions to diagnose.** Inelastic means no response; the firm that sends the payment bears all the burden; a higher tax mechanically guarantees more revenue; the steeper-looking line is always less elastic regardless of scale.

**Boundary and ethics.** Specify competition, period, schedules, tax type, and what stays fixed. Remittance equivalence here excludes differences in compliance, salience, liquidity, and enforcement. Burden on buyers/sellers is not a full distributional analysis of workers, owners, households, or public spending. Neither incidence nor tax revenue determines fairness. [F9, F11, F15]

**Useful native practice.** Percentage matching, typed midpoint calculation with rounding specified, before/after price sorting, and a changed-remitter transfer. Fieldwork: distinguish “who sends it,” “who responds,” and “who ends up worse off.”

### u06 — Who is missing from the decision?

**Source-supported core.** A private choice can impose costs or benefits not incorporated in its decision. Social marginal accounting adds the relevant outside effects rather than treating the private margin as complete. [F13, F17] Measurement and implementation limit taxes, rules, and compensation; efficient allocation and fair distribution are separate evaluations. [F14, F15]

**Original scenarios.** A delivery depot schedules extra late runs. Residents outside the buyer–seller deal bear a specified noise cost; the fictional schedule shows how including that cost changes the selected quantity (N9). A tenant pays for a shared drainage improvement that also benefits neighbors, providing a positive-spillover contrast. Held-back transfer: a team chooses among noise limits, a measured per-event charge, and a jointly negotiated quiet-hours arrangement; the keyed task is identifying missing implementation facts, not electing a universal winner.

**Close contrasts.** A supplier's own fuel expense is a private cost. A customer's agreed payment is a transfer within the transaction, not itself an unpriced external harm. A neighbor's loss of custom due solely to normal price competition should not automatically be treated as the same mechanism as physical pollution. For that last contrast, explain the narrower mechanism instead of making a sweeping assertion that price effects never matter.

**Misconceptions to diagnose.** A voluntary purchase has no third-party effects; every externality requires the same policy; a calculated total gain means every person gains; a charge automatically compensates the harmed people.

**Boundary and ethics.** State which effects are included, their units, the decision horizon, and whether quantities can be changed. Money-valued damage is a model input, not the worth of a person or a universal willingness to tolerate harm. A benefit–cost total does not decide rights, acceptable risk, or compensation. Ostrom's research motivates asking how local rules fit the setting, not assuming community governance always succeeds. [F16]

**Useful native practice.** Map affected parties, distinguish private/external/transfer entries, add marginal costs once, compare a changed spillover, and diagnose missing implementation information. Fieldwork: identify one affected person absent from an everyday transaction and one way their interests could enter the decision.

## Teaching method and authoring constraints

Read in full: `agora/app/CONTENT_SCHEMA.md` and `agora/research/skepsis-learning-science.md`. Use the actual inherited pipeline: ungraded first attempt, concise principle and worked example, authored inline checks, harder retrieval, held-back transfer, matching, and later spaced return. The old Skepsis research file contains secondary summaries and unreverified effect sizes; it guides continuity, but those statistics should not become new Oikos claims.

- Preserve four lessons per unit with a meaningful fourth boundary/integration lesson, not a collection of trivia. Each lesson needs a taught principle, linked application, and a genuinely new transfer situation.
- Teach every term, arithmetic convention, and condition before scored use. A graph is optional support; offer a compact table/text equivalent. Start with the resource or human decision before symbols.
- Use the now-supported `model` status for conditional theoretical relationships; `evidence` for bounded observations; `practice` for a procedure; `ethics` for stated normative commitments; `fictional` for invented cases/calculations. A model can be useful without being a measured finding.
- For numerical answers, include units, signs, bounds, tax type, period, counterfactual, and whether refunds/fees are already included. Each num needs 2–3 authored plausible wrong calculations in distractors; no random or borrowed numbers. Avoid false precision; accept stated rounding and meaningful aliases only.
- Vary four-option keys and lengths naturally. Author plausible misconceptions and their repairs. Do not reuse a neighboring true economic principle as a false tile or a generic wrong answer.
- Cold transfers must follow a same-lesson principle and remain absent from intros. Include “insufficient information” only when the stem genuinely leaves the claimed conclusion unidentified.
- No timing for policy tradeoffs, stakeholder judgments, or complex calculations. Numbers are excluded from speed unless explicitly marked speed:true; leave all multistep examples unmarked. Simple vocabulary or one-step arithmetic may be eligible only when appropriate.
- Score arithmetic and conditional reasoning. Use reflection or branching cases for competing defensible values. A learner should never earn a moral-character score or be told that efficiency proves fairness.

## Source-use cautions and handoff

OpenStax sometimes phrases model results too broadly for this course. In particular, do not turn its elasticity chapter's “always” language into a claim covering every demand setting, and do not equate inelastic demand with zero quantity response. Its positive/normative discussion elsewhere calls a project “worthy” after a benefit–cost comparison; Oikos should instead state the objective and included valuations explicitly before drawing a recommendation. These are reasons to author careful original examples, not to treat textbook wording as an answer key.

The Nobel site returned access errors for the lecture PDFs. Holmström's actual lecture was successfully opened through MIT's author publication page; Ostrom's revised draft was successfully opened through Indiana University's institutional repository. No unsupported claim of reading the inaccessible versions is made. The Stanford-hosted 1991 multitasking paper also failed to load; it is background provenance in Holmström's lecture, not a separate verified ledger source.

Use original prose, scenarios, numbers, and visuals; do not copy textbook exercises or illustrations. New claims about real policies, real elasticities, or a specific institution's legal constraints require separate verification before authoring. This research proposes the six-unit progression and boundaries; it does not prescribe answers to unresolved normative choices.
