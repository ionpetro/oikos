## Six-unit sequence

### u07 — Commons: Build cooperation without a magic formula

**Sources:** I1–I3. Distinguish incentives in a specified game from a prediction of universal selfishness. Also distinguish the resource's characteristics from who owns or governs it. Ostrom's evidence supports the possibility of effective user governance and warns against a universal institutional recipe.

1. **One person's gain, everyone's problem:** read a contribution payoff and distinguish individual from group returns. Use a shared translation library or community notice system.
2. **What kind of shared thing?** Teach rivalry and exclusion with explicit capacity/access facts. Use *pure public good* for non-rival and non-excludable; label an excludable non-rival service a club good. CORE's broader terminology is disclosed in I2.
3. **Rules people can use:** inspect membership, resource limits, monitoring, dispute resolution and who can revise the rules in a fictional irrigation cooperative. These are design questions, not sufficient conditions for success.
4. **When the boundary changes:** the same shared resource becomes congested, outsiders join, or the stock falls. Reassess a rule rather than assuming communication, privatization or state ownership always solves it.

**Numerical prototype:** Four teams each have 10 tokens. Each contributed token gives 0.5 tokens to *each* team, including its contributor; contributions are simultaneous, with no repeat rounds or other rewards. If all contribute 10, each receives 20 rather than retaining 10. Holding others' actions fixed, one extra contribution costs its contributor 1 and returns 0.5, but returns 2 to the group: private marginal change −0.5; group marginal change +1. This is a model of a tension, not advice to free-ride. Change the private return to 1.2 and the same free-rider conclusion no longer follows.

**Misconceptions/boundaries:** public good = government service; common property = unregulated access; all cooperation requires altruism; monitoring eliminates conflict. Do not classify a crowded free-access car park as non-rival. Reflection: who should help set a resource limit, and whose burden is missing?

### u08 — Information: What the other side knows

**Sources:** I4–I5. Hidden attributes can change the pool of trading partners. Hidden actions concern choices that are difficult to observe or verify. Neither label establishes bad character, and an unfavorable result does not identify its cause.

1. **Before the exchange:** a buyer cannot observe refurbished-device quality; a pooled offer can make reliable sellers withdraw.
2. **After the agreement:** distinguish hidden quality at sale from unobserved maintenance after contracting; separate hidden action from bad luck.
3. **Make a claim checkable:** compare an independent inspection with a seller's unsupported badge; inspect what a guarantee actually covers and who can fulfill it. Information reduces uncertainty only to its verified extent.
4. **Information has limits and costs:** a diagnostic report resolves battery condition but not future software support. A proportionate test can be useful without demanding unrelated personal information.

**Numerical prototype:** Equal numbers of two device types are initially offered. Buyers value high-quality devices at $900 and low-quality ones at $300; sellers require at least $700 and $200 respectively. Buyers are risk-neutral and cannot identify types. The initial expected value is $600, below the high-quality sellers' threshold. If they leave, the remaining pool's value is $300, not $600. Verified type information restores a possible $700–$900 bargaining interval for a high-quality device. No transaction is compulsory, and inspection costs are omitted only because the model says so.

**Misconceptions/boundaries:** every unknown is adverse selection; moral hazard means immorality; privacy means deceit; a costly signal necessarily proves quality. A fully observed quality difference is not this hidden-attribute problem. Reflection: what information is necessary, and what should remain private?

### u09 — Power: When alternatives are thin

**Sources:** I6–I7. Examine feasible substitutes, switching, entry and control of access. Employer power can arise through search frictions or job attributes even with many employers; concentration alone is an incomplete diagnosis. Compare institutional options without converting a model into a verdict about current law.

1. **A choice on paper:** compare a portable subscription with one whose migration process blocks switching. Ask which alternatives are usable at the relevant time.
2. **Profit is not total gains:** compare seller profit, buyer surplus and total modeled surplus under explicitly supplied quantities and valuations.
3. **Power on either side:** use the buyer of a specialist service or labor as the decision-maker; distinguish one seller from one effective buyer and recognize countervailing negotiation.
4. **Big does not settle it:** compare a large provider with open entry and easy switching against a smaller provider controlling a necessary input. Assess remedies against the diagnosed barrier and their implementation costs.

**Numerical prototype:** A fictional seller has constant cost $20 per unit, no fixed cost, no spillovers, and two buyers each wanting at most one unit, valued at $70 and $50. At a uniform price of $70, the high-value buyer accepts: profit $50, buyer surplus $0, total surplus $50. At $50 both accept: profit $60, buyer surplus $20, total surplus $80. Do **not** call $70 the profit-maximizing monopoly price in this example: it is not. The exercise teaches comparison and the error of equating a higher price with higher profit. A later model could change the second valuation and change the maximizing choice.

**Misconceptions/boundaries:** large = unlawful; revenue = social benefit; more firms automatically means easy switching; every minimum-wage change has the same employment effect. Reflection: which tradeoffs matter if interoperability improves choice but introduces real transition costs?

### u10 — Work: Reward the job, not just the count

**Sources:** I5, I8–I9. This extends, rather than repeats, introductory incentives: multiple tasks, noisy outcomes, incomplete contracting, allocation of risk and metric design. Holmström explains why paying more for an easily measured activity can divert effort from valuable competing work. Goodhart is a diagnostic family of risks, not a ban on measurement.

1. **The job is larger than the measure:** ticket closures can omit quality, unresolved needs or later rework.
2. **Effort, results and risk:** distinguish an agent's controllable action from a result affected by demand or equipment failure. Ask what evidence is informative before attributing blame.
3. **The target changes behavior:** compare measurement for learning with a reward rule that makes an incomplete proxy worth optimizing. A balanced dashboard also needs scrutiny; adding metrics is not a guarantee.
4. **When a measure works:** contrast a verified measure aligned with a narrowly specified task with the same metric transplanted into a broader job. Review incentives with the people doing and receiving the work.

**Numerical prototype:** Two fictional service methods each use one day. A closes 20 tickets, of which 8 reopen; B closes 16, of which 2 reopen. Assume each reopening indicates failure to resolve that ticket, no duplicate tickets and no other quality difference. Durable resolutions: A=12, B=14. A $2 payment per closure yields $40 versus $32, so the metric rewards A more despite fewer durable resolutions. This does not estimate the response to a real pay policy. If an independent check finds zero reopenings in both methods, the earlier quality objection changes.

**Misconceptions/boundaries:** all pay for performance fails; outputs reveal effort; workers are uniformly selfish; one improved metric eliminates gaming. Reflection: which important work is invisible in a metric you use, and how can its users contest an inaccurate record?

### u11 — Distribution: Bigger for whom?

**Sources:** I10–I13. Pareto improvement, greater total monetary surplus and a preferred distribution are different claims. A distributional description and a justice criterion must be visible before asking for a judgment. Do not treat every transfer as a new resource cost, or treat its being a transfer as morally irrelevant.

1. **Totals can hide losers:** report individual changes alongside a total; distinguish actual compensation from a claim that winners could compensate losers.
2. **Name the fairness criterion:** compare equal shares, need, contribution and a legitimate process; score the application of a stated rule, not the choice of one universal rule.
3. **Follow the resource and the payment:** distinguish administration, real resource use, funding flows and who ultimately gains or loses. Fix the comparison boundary.
4. **Two reasonable rankings:** contrast options that trade total resources against the position of the worse-off group. A different ethical priority can change the preferred option without changing arithmetic.

**Numerical prototypes:** With no outside effects and money as each person's sole payoff, A=(60,40) and B=(90,20). Totals rise 100→110, but the second person loses 20. It is not a Pareto improvement. If B includes an actual transfer of 20 from the first to the second with zero transfer cost, the resulting (70,40) is a Pareto improvement over A. Do not claim this makes it uniquely fair. Separately, $100 transferred from an included funder to an included recipient cancels in the simple aggregate cash ledger, while $10 of additional real administration does not; the recipient still receives a meaningful benefit. Specify that there are no behavioral responses in this simplified accounting example.

**Misconceptions/boundaries:** efficient = fair; average gain = everybody gains; equal income = equal need; potential compensation = payment. Avoid a Gini drill unless its normalization is taught. Reflection: which distributional facts would change your assessment while leaving the total unchanged?

### u12 — Evidence: Did it work, for whom, and why?

**Sources:** I13–I14. Separate forecasts from evaluated outcomes, a comparison from its causal assumptions, and attribution from a decision to scale. Choose a method for the question and retain uncertainty.

1. **What would have happened anyway?** Contrast a raw before/after change with a justified comparison.
2. **Read the comparison:** work through randomized assignment and nonrandom comparison limits; calculation is not identification.
3. **Process, outcome and cost:** check implementation and whether the measure addresses the intended result. Record who benefits and who is missing.
4. **A result is not a universal license:** a credible local estimate still needs uncertainty, costs, access, spillovers and context checked before expansion.

**Numerical prototype:** Fictional course-completion rates move from 50% to 70% in a pilot group and from 45% to 55% in a comparison group. The difference-in-differences calculation is (70−50)−(55−45)=10 percentage points; the pilot's raw change is 20 points. Causal interpretation requires a credible common untreated trend and no other differential changes; two displayed periods alone cannot establish those conditions. A companion randomized-offer case reports 60/100 completions versus 50/100 by original assigned group: 10 percentage points, without claiming significance from the difference alone. All figures are invented.

**Misconceptions/boundaries:** correlation proves impact; random assignment guarantees identical realized groups; “not significant” proves no effect; one successful trial automatically scales. Fieldwork: specify one decision, outcome, comparison, uncertainty and revision trigger before collecting results.

## Authoring handoff

The sequence aligns with root's subjects `commons information power work distribution evidence`. u07 needs prior marginal/private-social reasoning; u08 prepares incomplete-contract work; u09 supplies feasible-alternative analysis; u10 applies incentives across tasks; u11 separates allocation from evaluation criteria; u12 tests interventions against counterfactuals and stated goals. Numerical drafts above were checked arithmetically. Authoring must still apply the finalized course curriculum and independently review every keyed option, source assignment and deciding phrase.

Do not import the sources' historical percentages, current legal thresholds, or sensational real-world examples merely to add authority. No new empirical effect size is necessary for these units. Where source language is stronger than the mechanism establishes, teach the conditional mechanism and state the limit. The two independent content reviews should include a search for false universals, unacknowledged model assumptions, moral rankings hidden in answer keys, inappropriate inference from bad outcomes, and comparison/denominator errors.
